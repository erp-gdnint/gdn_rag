GDAC MENTORSHIP PROGRAMME

Risk and escalation FAQ and guide

Potential challenges to watch for and what to do if you face one

This guide brings together the most common challenges encountered across GDAC grant cycles. It is structured around six themes, grouping related challenges so the guidance is easier to apply. It is intended as a practical reference. The focus throughout is on what to do to address or overcome the challenge so that you can move forward with you grant and gain the most learning possible. The themes are:

Agreements, Compliance and Institutional Setup

Financial Planning and Reporting

The Advisor-Grantee Relationship

Communication and Escalation

Team Dynamics and Personal Capacity

External Risks and Timeline Pressures

| THEME 1 AGREEMENTS, COMPLIANCE AND INSTITUTIONAL SETUPChallenges that tend to arise before the project can begin |

A significant number of the most disruptive challenges in GDAC projects happen before any implementation begins. Most are preventable, but only if identified early.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Institutional and legal barriers | COMMON SCENARIOSThe host institution refuses to sign the grant agreement, or loses the legal right to receive foreign funds due to national regulations.The institution demands a higher overhead rate than what was budgeted in the proposal, triggering a renegotiation before the project has started.Internal approval chains are slower and less transparent than the grantee anticipated.WHAT HELPSIf your institution's legal capacity to sign international grant agreements or its overhead rate has not already been confirmed as part of the eligibility process, raise this with your institution immediately: this most commonly affects MIDP grantees.If any of these issues emerge after the award, raise them with GDN immediately rather than trying to resolve them quietly.GDN NOTESBudgets cannot be changed after award without a formal amendment process. Legal blocks are outside GDN's control, but early transparency gives everyone more time and often more options to respond. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Rescaling the project | COMMON SCENARIOSSecond and third prize ORD winners need to formally rescale their project to match their actual award amount, with mentor validation required within the first month.Disagreements between the grantee and mentor about what to cut can delay the start of implementation if they are not resolved quickly.WHAT HELPSTreat rescaling as a substantive task. Come to the first advisor meeting having already thought through realistic options for scope reduction.If there is disagreement about how to rescale, involve GDN before the month-one deadline rather than after.GDN NOTESAdvisor approval of the rescaled project is a formal requirement before GDN can proceed. It cannot be done retrospectively. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Ethics and regulatory approval | COMMON SCENARIOSInstitutional and national ethics approval is required before research fieldwork can begin, particularly for work involving surveys or vulnerable groups. This process can take several months and needs to be planned for accordingly.Ethics delays can deplete the budget during the waiting period, leaving a gap in funds when implementation actually starts.WHAT HELPSFind out which approvals are needed and from which bodies before the project starts, and begin the process as early as possible.Inform GDN and your advisor as soon as a delay is identified. Budget realignment may be possible if flagged early.GDN NOTESGDN can provide a letter of support to accompany ethics applications. |

| THEME 2 FINANCIAL PLANNING AND REPORTINGBudget gaps, reporting obligations, and financial risk |

Financial challenges tend to accumulate through overlooked budget lines, missed deadlines, or changes made without the right approvals. Most are avoidable with good planning and clear communication from the start.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Budget planning and unauthorised changes | COMMON SCENARIOSProposals omit key costs such as publication fees, ethics review, or dissemination. These cannot be added after the award without a formal amendment.Changes to the budget, workplan, or team are made during implementation without prior approval from GDN and the advisor.WHAT HELPSUse the full project lifecycle as a budget checklist: proposal, ethics, implementation, reporting, and dissemination. Ask your advisor to review the budget before submission.Any change to the budget, workplan, or team requires approval from both your advisor and GDN before it is enacted, not after.Keep a record of all approved changes and the communications that confirmed them.GDN NOTESApproval is expected before changes are made, not after. Requesting it retroactively is often difficult and sometimes not possible, and can create compliance risks and delay disbursements. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| FUS and reporting obligations | COMMON SCENARIOSThe Financial Utilisation Statement (FUS) requires PI and institutional signatures. Universities can often take weeks to process this, which delays submission and holds up the next tranche of funding.An FUS is also required within 15 days of the end of every quarter, even when it is not tied to a disbursement milestone. This catches many grantees off guard.Progress reports frequently reach GDN without prior advisor review, which is a formal requirement and causes disbursement delays when it is skipped.WHAT HELPSStart the internal sign-off process for the FUS at least two to three weeks before it is due. Put quarterly FUS dates in your calendar at the start of the project.Share report drafts with your advisor at least two weeks before any GDN deadline. Advisor sign-off is not optional.GDN NOTESGDN will not process a disbursement based on a report that has not been approved by the advisor. Delays in reporting directly delay the project. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Financial mismanagement | COMMON SCENARIOSFunds are misused or misallocated, whether through, but not limited to,honest accounting errors or more serious cases. This is more common in organisations without established grant management systems.WHAT HELPSAssign financial responsibilities clearly within your team from day one and keep records of all transactions.Understand who holds financial authority within your institution: particularly at universities, where approval chains can be bureaucratic and outside the team's control.If your organisation has not managed a grant at this scale before, identify what financial systems need to be in place before implementation starts.GDN NOTESThis is one of the most serious risk categories. GDN may need to recover funds, and there can be legal implications. A proactive approach to financial management, and open communication with GDN from the earliest moment issues arise, is the strongest protection. |

| THEME 3 THE MENTOR-GRANTEE RELATIONSHIPExpectations, coordination, and how the relationship works in practice |

The mentor-grantee relationship is arguably the most valuable and most variable element of GDAC. Most difficulties in this relationship trace back to expectations or assumptions that were not made explicit at the start.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Role clarity and report coordination | COMMON SCENARIOSGrantees expect the advisor to rewrite reports, supervise the project, or take on decisions that belong to the grantee.Reports are submitted to GDN without the advisor having reviewed them first, which is a recurring cause of disbursement delays.Mentors provide feedback that is not acknowledged or acted on, leaving them uncertain whether their input is being used.WHAT HELPSSet expectations at the first meeting: how often you will communicate, what the advisor will review, and when drafts need to be shared before deadlines.The advisor's role is to help you think, not to do the work. Project decisions remain yours.When you receive feedback, acknowledge it. If you cannot act on something, explain why. Not responding at all is the most damaging option.GDN NOTESCoordination problems between advisor and grantee are the single most common cause of disbursement delays in the programme. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Relationship difficulties among project teams | COMMON SCENARIOSMismatched working styles, communication differences, or unresolved frustrations create friction that slows the project.Power imbalances within the grantee's own team can affect the PI's ability to lead and are not always visible from the outside.WHAT HELPSAddress friction early. Tensions that go unacknowledged tend to compound, particularly under deadline pressure.If a pattern of difficulty persists, bring it to GDN before it affects the work. GDN is available to mediate.If something within your own team is affecting your ability to lead the project, name it to GDN. You do not have to manage it alone.GDN NOTESGDN has mediated relationship difficulties at various levels of complexity. Early escalation consistently leads to better outcomes than waiting. |

| THEME 4 COMMUNICATION AND ESCALATIONWhen to reach out and why timing matters |

Across almost every difficult case in GDAC's history, the problem was known earlier than it was raised. The guidance in this section is straightforward but makes a significant difference in practice.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Late or absent escalation | COMMON SCENARIOSProblems are not reported when they arise. Grantees attempt to resolve things internally, often out of concern about how it will reflect on them.By the time issues reach GDN, options have narrowed and the situation is harder to resolve for everyone.WHAT HELPSThe earlier a problem is raised, the more options there are. GDN has seen a wide range of challenges and there is no situation too complicated or uncomfortable to raise.A short message to GDN or your mentor is enough to open a conversation. You do not need to have all the answers before reaching out.GDN NOTESThere is no penalty for raising a concern early. Waiting, however, tends to narrow your options and make problems harder to resolve. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Overcoming language barriers | COMMON SCENARIOSFor grantees working in non-English speaking contexts, conducting high quality work across multiple language can add complexity to the work throughout the grant cycle: in reporting, in conversations with mentors, and in reading GDN documentation.Language gaps in reports are sometimes read as conceptual gaps, which can lead to misunderstandings during review.WHAT HELPSLeverage the plethora of online tools available, such as Google translate and also Grammarly, etc. to help you check translations and refine your writing in English. If language is a genuine barrier, s If you feel any reasonable adjustments might help you succeed (e.g. requesting colleagues to speak slowly on calls or including closed captions on fast-paced larger group virtual meetings), say so early to your mentor and GDN. It is your responsibility to proactively flag and problem-solve for these, and it is easier to find solutions at the start than to correct misunderstandings or catch up later.Consider having reports reviewed by a colleague for clarity before sharing with your mentor. Mentors: distinguish between language issues and gaps in understanding when reviewing work from non-native speakers and ask more questions of the grantee if needed to understand this distinction.GDN NOTESGDN's work is grounded in LMIC contexts and language diversity is part of that. The goal is never perfect English; it is clear enough communication that the work gets the support it deserves. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Advisor unavailability | COMMON SCENARIOSMentors become unresponsive or unavailable due to personal or professional circumstances. These situations are handled on a case-by-case basis by GDN, depending on each situation. Grantees are often unsure whether to wait or to contact GDN, and may lose weeks before escalating.WHAT HELPSIf there are significant gaps in your advisor's responsiveness, contact GDN. Do not wait over 2 weeks at most before raising it.Mentors: if your availability is about to change, let the grantee and GDN know in advance.GDN NOTESGDN will step in to find solutions, including identifying interim support where possible. |

| THEME 5 TEAM DYNAMICS AND PERSONAL CAPACITYChanges within the team and the people running the project |

Projects are run by people whose circumstances sometimes change. These situations are not unusual and are not always negative for the individuals involved. However, they can lead to disruptions and what matters is how they are communicated and managed.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Team conflict and role changes | COMMON SCENARIOSDisagreements within the team over direction, workload, or responsibilities slow implementation.A team member leaves or changes role mid-project, reducing capacity or creating gaps in expertise.WHAT HELPSEstablish clear roles and responsibilities before implementation begins. Revisit these if the team composition changes.Any significant change to the team needs to be discussed with and approved by GDN and the mentor before it is finalised.Address internal conflicts early rather than hoping they resolve themselves under pressure.GDN NOTESTeam changes require formal approval. Unannounced departures or replacements create compliance issues and can delay disbursements and implementation progress. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Personal capacity and wellbeing | COMMON SCENARIOSThe PI receives a significant new opportunity or promotion mid-project, reducing their available time.A grantee becomes overwhelmed and withdraws from communication, which can put the project at risk if it goes on long enough.WHAT HELPSIf your availability is going to change, tell your advisor and GDN early so adjustments can be made.If you are struggling, reach out to your advisor or GDN. Silence tends to make things harder.Mentors: notice when a grantee has gone quiet and check in on them directly.GDN NOTESGDN has supported grantees through difficult periods and adjusted timelines where needed. The goal is always to help the project succeed, not to penalise difficulty or stifle growth opportunities, particularly if they are related the work and success of the GDAC grant. |

| THEME 6 EXTERNAL RISKS AND TIMELINE PRESSURESForces outside anyone's control and how to prepare |

Some challenges cannot be prevented or controlled. What can be managed is how prepared everyone is and how quickly the right people are informed when something happens.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| External crises | COMMON SCENARIOSPolitical instability, conflict, or other major external disruption prevents project activity or creates safety concerns.Projects working in fragile contexts or with vulnerable communities face compounded risk when external conditions deteriorate.WHAT HELPSIf your context carries elevated risk, build a buffer into your timeline and agree with GDN on how you will communicate if something happens.If a crisis occurs, prioritise safety first. Inform GDN and your advisor as soon as the project is affected.GDN NOTESGDN's primary tools in a genuine crisis are flexibility, empathy, and no-cost extensions. GDN has not abandoned a grantee facing a real external crisis. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Cumulative delays and extended timelines | COMMON SCENARIOSSmall delays in reporting, approvals, and signatures accumulate across the lifecycle, turning a 12-month project into a significantly longer one.Mentors committed to a fixed engagement period find their involvement extending beyond what they originally agreed to.WHAT HELPSReview your project timeline regularly and flag emerging gaps early, not after deadlines are missed.Let your mentor know about any extensions as early as possible, with context.GDN NOTESGDN sets contracts at 18 months by default and uses a 12+X month structure to absorb smaller delays. These buffers are not unlimited. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Post-project obligations | COMMON SCENARIOSThe grant agreement includes monitoring obligations that extend up to two years after the project ends, including an annual grantee survey. Many grantees are not aware of this until they receive a request.Publication commitments, including submitting at least one paper to a peer-reviewed journal, also carry beyond the formal end of the grant.WHAT HELPSRead Annex III of your grant agreement carefully before signing. Post-project obligations are binding.Build your publication timeline into planning during implementation rather than leaving it entirely to closeout.GDN NOTESGDN conducts annual surveys with grantees for up to two years post-project. Grantees who stay in contact with GDN after their grant tend to benefit from alumni opportunities including reviewer roles and future collaboration. |
