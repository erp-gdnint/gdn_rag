GDAC MENTORSHIP PROGRAMME

Grantee onboarding pack

Everything you need to get started and to make the most of your mentor relationship

Welcome to GDAC. This pack is your starting point: a clear picture of how the programme works, what is expected of you, and how to get the most out of your relationship with your mentor and GDN, where GDN and your mentor share responsibility for the quality of the grant process, while your team retains full responsibility for the quality of your outputs. The mentorship you receive could well be one of the most valuable opportunities that you have to learn and grow during your journey as a grantee. You can hear more about [why mentors serve in their roles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1e2Hft3aivc&t=14s) and how grantees have been supported and transformed by their mentorship experiences.

Read through this before your first meeting with your advisor. It covers the programme structure, your advisor's role (and yours), key reporting requirements, and what to do when challenges arise.

1. The GDAC programme

GDAC, the Global Development Awards Competition, is a GDN initiative that funds outstanding research and development projects in low- and middle-income countries. Grants are structured around a clear lifecycle, with payments tied to milestones and a dedicated advisor assigned to every project.

Your grant sits within one of three programme tracks:

| ORDJapanese Award for Outstanding Research on DevelopmentResearch-oriented projects. Your advisor is a Scientific and Technical Advisor with academic expertise. | MIDPJapanese Award for Most Innovative Development ProjectImplementation-oriented projects run by NGOs. Your advisor is a Technical Advisor with regional or sector expertise. | JSDFJapan Social Development Fund AwardCommunity-focused initiatives. Your advisor is a Technical Advisor. |

GDN defines [mentorship](https://www.gdn.int/mentoring-gdn) as a three-way relationship between you, your advisor, and the GDN program manager. All three parties have specific roles. Understanding this triangle from the start often prevents the most common sources of confusion in the programme.

4. Working with your mentor

Your mentor commits approximately four working days across the life of the project. This is not a lot, which is why the relationship works best when it is structured and focused from the start. Expect more intensive involvement at the beginning (reviewing your plan, co-developing the communication plan) and around key reporting milestones.

Your first month together

Within the first month after signing your contract, GDN will send a three-way introduction email connecting you, your mentor, and your GDN program manager. This kicks off [a meeting](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1O-Mk-It7ioy2SinHti7n8xBVzzGpdqPp/edit?usp=sharing&ouid=116394735110013719331&rtpof=true&sd=true) where you will:

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Co-develop your communication planAgree on how often you will meet, in what format, what you will share, and what the advisor expects to review before deadlines. GDN will provide a template to get you started. | Review and approve the project planYour advisor will review your project design and methodology. For 2nd and 3rd prize ORD winners, this includes validating the rescaled project to match your actual award amount. |

Setting up your communication plan

The communication plan is not a formality. It is what prevents the most common problems. Cover these four questions explicitly:

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| How often will we meet?Set a regular rhythm, monthly or at key milestones, rather than only reaching out when something goes wrong. | What will I share, and when?Agree what drafts and updates go to your advisor before finalising. Reports must always go to your advisor before GDN, allow at least two weeks. |

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| What happens if something goes wrong?Agree an escalation approach before you need it. If your advisor is unreachable, or a problem emerges, what is the first step? | What does a good meeting look like?Share an agenda in advance. Come with specific questions or decisions, not just a general update. Your advisor's time is limited; use it well. |

|  |
| :-: |
| TWO RULES OF THUMB THAT PREVENT MANY PROBLEMSYour advisor should never be surprised by a report that has already been sent to GDN. Share drafts at least two weeks before any deadline: this is a formal requirement, not a courtesy.When you receive feedback, acknowledge it. If you disagree or cannot act on a recommendation, say so. Silence is the most damaging response in this relationship. |

5. MEL basics: key concepts to know

Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) is built into every GDAC grant. Many misunderstandings during implementation, and most reporting problems, trace back to different interpretations of these core concepts. Getting these right early makes everything else easier.

GDN is developing a set of MEL-related micro-explanations that will walk through each concept in more depth. The definitions below are the shared starting point.

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| Term | What it means in practice |
| Output | The direct, tangible result of your activities: what you produce. Example: a training delivered, a survey completed, a policy brief published. |
| Outcome | The change that results from your outputs. Example: participants apply new skills; a policy brief shapes a government decision. Outcomes take time and are harder to measure directly. |
| Indicator | A specific, measurable way of tracking progress toward an output or outcome. A good indicator is defined and agreed from the start, not constructed retrospectively at reporting stage. |
| Beneficiary | The people your project is designed to help, defined precisely and agreed with GDN early. Vague definitions create significant problems at reporting and portfolio comparison. |
| Baseline | The starting point against which you measure change. Should be data-grounded, not aspirational. If your target cannot be tracked from a clear starting point, revisit it before implementation. |
| Theory of Change (ToC) | The logic connecting your activities to your outcomes. Being able to explain this clearly, to your mentor, to GDN, and to your beneficiaries, is a sign of a well-designed project. |

7. When things get difficult

Implementation challenges are a normal part of GDAC. They can be frustrating and stressful, and they are also an opportunity to learn. What often matters most in term of a timely and positive outcome is how early these challenges are surfaced. GDN has navigated almost every kind of difficulty; the ones that become serious are almost always the ones raised too late.

|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| If you are struggling with... | Talk to... | When |
| Research direction or methodology | Your advisor first | As soon as a question arises: do not wait for a scheduled meeting |
| A budget issue, missing cost, or financial question | GDN directly (awards@gdn.int) | Immediately: budgets cannot be changed retroactively |
| Delays in institutional approvals or ethics | GDN — and keep your advisor informed | As soon as the delay is confirmed, not after the deadline is missed |
| A difficult dynamic with your advisor | GDN, confidentially | Early. Before it becomes a pattern that affects the work |
| A change in your team, workplan, or budget | Your advisor first, then GDN for approval | Before making the change, not after |
| A personal or health situation affecting your capacity | GDN, confidentially | As soon as you are able to: there is no penalty for transparency |
| An external crisis (conflict, legal, access barriers) | GDN immediately | As soon as it affects the project |
