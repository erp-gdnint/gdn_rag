GDAC MENTORSHIP PROGRAMME

Mentor onboarding sheet

A practical guide to your role, your deliverables, and how to make the relationship work

This sheet is a concise reference for mentors[1](#footnote1). It covers what the role involves, what you are expected to deliver, and what to do if problems arise. It is not a substitute for your Terms of Reference or contract, both of which you should read carefully.

1. What this role is, and what it is not

GDN defines mentorship as a three-way relationship between the grantee, the advisor, and the GDN program manager. All three parties have a role in the success of the project. Your job as mentor is to provide an open window of independent peer review and expert guidance, rather than to manage the project, supervise the team, or take responsibility for outputs.

4. Working with your grantee

At the kickoff meeting, facilitated by GDN, you and the grantee will co-develop a communication plan. GDN provides a template for this. The plan covers how often you will meet, how documents will be shared, and what happens if communication breaks down. It is submitted to GDN as part of your month-one report.

A few things that consistently make the relationship work well:

Set expectations at the first meeting. Grantees sometimes arrive expecting a level of supervision closer to a PhD relationship. Being clear early about what you will and will not do prevents frustration on both sides.

Acknowledge the context. Many grantees are working in demanding institutional environments, under time pressure, sometimes in a second language. What looks like a gap in understanding is sometimes a gap in bandwidth.

Respond to drafts within a reasonable timeframe. Delays on your end directly delay the grantee's disbursement. If you need more time, let the grantee and GDN know.

If the grantee goes quiet, reach out directly. Silence is often a sign of difficulty, not disengagement.
